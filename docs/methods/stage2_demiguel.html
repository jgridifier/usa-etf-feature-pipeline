For Jared: correction, and why KWZ was dropped. The research plan described Kan, Wang & Zhou (2022) as "an optimal blend of 1/N and a mean-variance rule that falls back to 1/N under noise." That is not what the paper says. Its rule is ŵ = ŵGMV + (ĉ/γ)·ŵz, and under noise it falls back to the global minimum-variance portfolio, not to equal weight. CIO and CoS dropped it from the holdout for three reasons:
  1. Wrong anchor. It falls back to minimum variance, which our earlier tests already found loses to equal weight on this panel.
  2. Infeasible here. It needs more observations than funds (h > N+3). With 45–96 funds, a 36-month lookback is impossible for every N, and 60 months is impossible from N = 60 up. On 156 weekly returns, the estimated ĉ was only 0.01–0.19, so the rule is essentially minimum variance.
  3. Our caps would change it into a different rule. On the development window, the raw KWZ weights carried about 290% in short positions on average. The long-only cap removes them, so what we could test would not be the paper's rule.
The EW-anchored blend the plan had in mind belongs to Tu & Zhou (2011). That paper predates the 2020+ rule, and no waiver was granted. The KWZ derivation, simulations and replication stay in the robustness appendix.

1. The rule in plain language

DeMiguel, Martín-Utrera, Nogales and Uppal (2020) start from equal weight across the eligible equity ETFs and tilt each fund's weight using two traits: 12-1 month momentum (MOM) and 12-month volatility (VOL). The formula is w = 1/N + X·θ/N. X holds each fund's standardized traits, and θ says how hard to lean on each one. Each month θ is re-picked from a fixed grid by asking which tilt would have done best over the last 24 months on return, minus a risk charge, minus a trading-cost penalty (κ = 5 times the cost). The paper's headline is that trading costs increase the number of traits worth using, because trades for different traits partly cancel. We reproduced that direction on public industry data: 1 significant trait with no costs, 2 at 50 bp.

Placebo. Each fund was given another fund's traits, which keeps how the traits are spread across funds and how they evolve, but breaks the link to the fund's own returns. Across 100 such runs, the frozen rule's real result had a p-value of 0.08. The fixes scored 0.70–0.85, no better than noise. In-sample, the real traits do beat the placebo (p = 0.01). Out of sample, the edge over EW sits inside noise.

The fixes. We tried the paper's quadratic (price-impact) cost formulation and ridge shrinkage of θ, with the strength of each chosen by cross-validation on past data only. They kept θ off the grid edge more often but traded more, because the chosen penalty itself jumped from month to month. They also scored lower. Under the pre-declared rule, the original κ = 5 stays frozen.

3. Turnover outcome and the CIO Book rule

A stage-2 candidate is Book-eligible only if both of these hold (CIO + CoS, set before the freeze):

  • (a) one-way turnover is at or below 100% a year on the development window;
  • (b) its CAGR stays above the static core's with costs doubled to 10 bp.

The frozen rule fails both. Turnover is 178%, and CAGR is 12.3% against 16.6% for the core, which is -4.3 pp. All three fixes fail too. A holdout PASS would be a research result only, not Book-eligible.

4. The holdout gate and how hard it is

There is one run over 2008-01 to 2016-10 (T = 106), charged 5 bp one-way. The candidate must clear all of these:

  • the composition tripwires;
  • C1: higher Sharpe than capped EW on a one-sided Memmel test with p ≤ 0.20;
  • C2: at least match LW minimum variance;
  • C3: drawdown no more than 5 points worse than EW, with the 2008-09 drawdown shown beside EW and the core;
  • C4: deflated Sharpe ratio of at least 0.95.

The holdout window has been used before. FT-MED (from 2002-07), RR-ERC (from 2003-06), Regime-dual (from 2004-11) and the Spectral RP sleeve (from 1998-02) all evaluated returns over 2008–2016, along with their nulls. We already know EW beat LW MinVar from 2002 on (0.524 vs 0.366 above BIL). The holdout is lightly used, not untouched, so every DSR above should be read with that in mind.

Power. Assume EW's true Sharpe is 0.55 and the run has N = 1. A rule that is truly no better than EW passes C1 and C4 about 11% of the time; under the old point-estimate C1 that figure was 26%. A rule that is truly 0.3 better passes about 81% of the time, falling to about 60% if 2008–2016 was harder on equal-weight-like portfolios (true EW Sharpe 0.35).

5. Holdout risk: the 2009 momentum crash

The frozen rule leans positive on momentum (θMOM ≈ +1). In public Ken French data the momentum factor fell 39% in March 2009, 45% in April and 21% in May. Over those three months the past-loser decile gained 158% while past winners gained 7%. ETF-level spreads are far smaller than single-stock spreads, but spring 2009 is the most likely place for this rule to fail C1 or C3. That risk was stated before the run.
